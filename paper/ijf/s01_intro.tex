\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A large share of Indian agricultural produce is traded through regulated wholesale markets (\emph{mandis}), although much is also sold outside them.
Each mandi publishes a daily minimum, maximum and modal price per commodity and variety through AGMARKNET, mirrored on the Open Government Data platform.
These reports provide public price information for farmers comparing sale timing and markets.

Forecasting them has a large literature (Section~\ref{sec:related}), dominated by studies of one market, a few markets, or monthly indices.
These studies typically use a single proportional hold-out, report deep or hybrid models against ARIMA, and rarely report naive baselines or prediction intervals \cite{paul2022brinjal,manogna2025deep,kumar2021onion}.
Forecast-evaluation research shows why this matters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item simple benchmarks are hard to beat \cite{makridakis2018statistical,makridakis2020m4};
  \item scaled errors are needed across heterogeneous series \cite{hyndman2006another};
  \item the point forecast that is optimal depends on the scoring function \cite{gneiting2011making,kolassa2016evaluating};
  \item leakage and data vintage can dominate conclusions \cite{hewamalage2022pitfalls,bokun2023fredsd}.
\end{itemize}

We ask how much of the apparent skill in mandi price forecasting survives an evaluation that is leakage-resistant, baseline-honest, provenance-audited and assessed on later reports.
Our contributions are empirical, and we state them with their limits.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A national-scale, leakage-resistant benchmark} of daily mandi modal-price forecasts.
    It covers \num{11845} market--commodity--variety series of 36 commodities at four horizons, with chronological target-purged splits.
    It includes five naive and seasonal baselines, a per-series exponential smoother and a global gradient-boosting baseline.
    Results are reported with MASE, Diebold--Mariano tests and per-series win rates.
  \item \textbf{The scoring function is decisive.} Between 17\% and 34\% of target changes are exactly zero, because mandis re-quote unchanged prices.
    Under absolute error, squared-loss global learners do not beat persistence at the next-report horizon, whereas an absolute-loss learner does.
    This is a predictable consequence of elicitation theory \cite{gneiting2011making}. We quantify it at scale for this domain.
  \item \textbf{A data-provenance effect.} A public mirror of AGMARKNET holds only 30\% of the official reports from October 2025 onwards.
    On the mirror, forecasts appear to degrade and conformal intervals appear to lose calibration after a change of the publishing portal.
    Scored on the same (series, origin) rows, the complete official record gives near-nominal coverage, although the 7-day gain over persistence still shrinks after the change. The gap statistics are consistent with a mechanism: the mirror's median gap between reports is three times longer.
  \item \textbf{Later-period retrospective evaluation.} Models trained on data up to 2023 are saved, and only then are six months of later official reports (April--October 2026) first downloaded and scored. This is a post-development hold-out, not a real-time evaluation, because the forecasts were not issued as the data arrived. We also run a retraining experiment whose final test period was not used for any retraining choice.
\end{enumerate}

Gains over persistence are modest: 3--8\% in MAE. We do not claim superiority over published models, whose data, horizons and protocols differ (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
The prototype system (single machine) is described in the supplementary material: Spark/Kafka/HDFS pipeline, API and a six-language farmer application.
